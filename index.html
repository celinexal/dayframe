Portfolio Pies
Understand, build and optimise your T212 pies
🥧 What is a pie?
T212's way of auto-investing across multiple stocks at once
A pie is a portfolio within your portfolio. You set a target allocation — e.g. 40% AXTI, 30% AAOI, 30% LITE — and T212 automatically splits every deposit or dividend across your stocks in those exact proportions. It removes emotion from investing: instead of manually deciding how much to put into each stock each time, the pie does it for you.
✅ Good for
Themes you want to hold long term · Drip-feeding money in regularly · Keeping diversification on autopilot
❌ Not ideal for
Short-term trades · Stocks you want to exit quickly · Anything where timing matters
💡 Pro tip
Build pies around themes — CPO supply chain, space economy, AI infrastructure — not random collections of stocks
🤖 How should I distribute my stocks?
Groq suggests a pie structure based on your current holdings
Press "Get suggestion" — Groq will analyse your holdings and suggest how to group them into pies and what allocation each stock should have

Layer 0 — Glass & Substrates
LPK
▼
Computer chips sit on a base material, similar to how a building sits on foundations
Traditional base materials expand and contract with heat at a different rate than chips — causing micro-cracks over time at very high speeds
Glass expands and contracts at nearly the same rate as chips, making it a much more stable foundation
This transition is just beginning and will take 2-3 years to become mainstream — it is a long-term setup, not a near-term trade
LPK

CEG · VST · GEV · PWR — nuclear is the only 24/7 solution
FOUNDATION
▼
A single large AI data centre uses as much electricity as a city of 50,000 people
Solar panels stop generating at night. Wind turbines stop when there is no wind. AI data centres need power every hour of every day without interruption
Nuclear power stations run continuously regardless of weather — this is why Microsoft, Google and Amazon are all signing long-term nuclear deals
Microsoft signed a 20-year contract with Constellation Energy (CEG) to buy the entire output of a restarted nuclear plant — 20 years of guaranteed income from one deal

ARM does not make chips — instead they design the instruction set that nearly every chip in the world is built around, and collect a small royalty each time one ships
Intel makes the general-purpose processors (CPUs) that handle everything in a data centre that is not pure AI — and as AI becomes more automated, data centres need far more of these too

Only three companies in the world make HBM: Micron, SK Hynix and Samsung — Micron's entire 2026 supply is already sold to NVIDIA

To make a laser chip, special crystal layers need to be grown one atom at a time in a high-precision vacuum chamber — this process is called epitaxy
The machines that do this are extremely specialised, cost millions each, and take months to install and qualify
These companies receive their orders and get paid before a single laser chip ever ships to a customer
Veeco specifically makes the type of machine (MBE) that AAOI uses for its highest-performance laser chips

AXTI ✓ · SOI
▼
Ordinary silicon cannot generate light, so it cannot be used to make laser chips — a special crystal called Indium Phosphide (InP) is required instead

When AAOI expands its factory, the demand for AXTI's crystals increases proportionally — the two are directly linked
Soitec (SOI) has a near-monopoly on a different specialist wafer used in the next generation of photonic chips — and each next-gen module needs four times more of it

What exactly is an ETF?
Plain English — no jargon
▼
An ETF (Exchange Traded Fund) is a single investment that holds hundreds of companies inside it
When you buy one share of a S&P 500 ETF, you instantly own a tiny piece of all 500 companies in the index — Apple, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Amazon and 496 others
If the market goes up, your ETF goes up. If it goes down, your ETF goes down. You get exactly the market average — no better, no worse
Think of it like buying a food hamper instead of individual items — you get a bit of everything in one purchase
What returns can you realistically expect?
Historical data — and the uncomfortable truth
▼
S&P 500 ETF — historical
~10%
average per year over 50 years. Some years -30%, some +30%.
Individual stocks — reality
90%
of individual stock pickers underperform the S&P 500 over 10+ years
The uncomfortable truth: most professional fund managers — people who do this full time with research teams and Bloomberg terminals — also fail to beat the S&P 500 consistently over a decade
That said, some individual stocks genuinely can outperform massively — AAOI, AXTI and AEHR in your portfolio have done exactly this. The challenge is finding them before they move, not after
The right mindset: the S&P 500 is the baseline everyone is trying to beat. If you are not beating it, you would have been better off buying the index
How the risk is completely different
This is the most important thing to understand
▼
An ETF holding 500 companies can only go to zero if all 500 companies fail simultaneously — this has never happened. The worst modern crash (2008) dropped the S&P 500 by 57%, but it recovered fully within 4 years
An individual stock can go to zero. It happens regularly — even at large, well-known companies. One piece of bad news, a fraud discovery or a product failure can permanently destroy the investment
The practical rule many investors follow: ETF as the foundation, individual stocks as the upside bet. If all your stock picks go wrong, the ETF keeps compounding. Your stocks are the bonus, not the safety net
✅ Your setup: Your T212 ISA holds a mix of individual stocks across specific themes. Adding a core ETF position (S&P 500 or Global All World) as a foundation would reduce the overall risk of the portfolio while keeping all your thematic upside.
Why people choose individual stocks over ETFs
The honest reasons
▼
You have specific insight: If you understand the photonics supply chain, spotted AXTI before it ran 5,000%, or know a sector better than the average investor — that is real edge that an ETF cannot give you
Theme exposure: The S&P 500 gives you about 4% exposure to semiconductor companies. If you believe photonics is the biggest infrastructure story of the decade, an ETF will barely move when AAOI runs 200%
Staying engaged: An underrated point — someone who researches their stocks and understands why they own them is less likely to panic-sell in a crash. Panic selling at the bottom is how most retail investors lose money
S&P 500 valuation: At certain times the index is expensive by historical measures, meaning future expected returns from the index are lower. Individual stocks in specific sectors may offer better risk/reward
